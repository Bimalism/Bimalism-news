Bimalism Desk · Markets Explainer

What the Sensex and Nifty numbers you scroll past actually mean

Most people see "Sensex up 400 points" in a headline and register it as vaguely good news without knowing what's actually being measured. The Sensex tracks 30 large, financially sound companies listed on the Bombay Stock Exchange; the Nifty 50 does the same for 50 companies on the National Stock Exchange. Neither index tells you what any individual stock did — a 1% rise in the Sensex can hide some stocks up 8% and others down 5%, averaged out by weight.

What actually moves these indices day to day is a mix of foreign institutional investment flows, RBI interest rate decisions, quarterly earnings season, global cues (US Fed decisions, crude oil prices), and domestic political stability. If you want to read market headlines with more context: a rate cut usually lifts banking and real estate stocks specifically; a weak rupee tends to help IT and export-heavy firms while hurting oil importers. The index number alone rarely tells that story — the sector breakdown does.

Bimalism Desk · Sports Explainer

Why IPL scheduling looks chaotic — and why it isn't

Fans often complain that IPL match timings and venue rotations seem arbitrary. They're not. The Indian Premier League's schedule is built around three competing constraints: broadcaster prime-time windows (matches are timed for peak TV and streaming viewership, not player convenience), venue availability across a compressed two-month window shared with state cricket associations, and travel logistics for ten franchises criss-crossing the country.

That's also why you'll see a cluster of afternoon matches on weekends (better live audience numbers) and why the same two cities rarely host back-to-back fixtures for the same team — travel days are budgeted tightly into the calendar. Understanding this makes the schedule easier to read: it's optimized for broadcast revenue and logistics first, and pure competitive fairness second.

Bimalism Desk · Technology Explainer

A plain-language glossary for the AI headlines you keep seeing

Tech headlines throw around terms that rarely get defined. A quick, honest glossary: a large language model (LLM) is software trained on huge amounts of text to predict and generate language — it doesn't "know" facts the way a database does, it predicts plausible text. Training is the one-time, expensive process of building the model; inference is every time you actually use it, which is comparatively cheap. A hallucination is when a model states something false with total confidence, because nothing in how it works distinguishes "confident and right" from "confident and wrong."

Multimodal means a model can handle more than text — images, audio, sometimes video. Fine-tuning means taking an already-trained model and further training it on a narrower dataset for a specific task. None of this requires a computer science degree to follow — most AI headlines reduce to one of these five ideas dressed up in company-specific branding.

Bimalism Desk · Reading the News

How to tell a slow news day from an important one

Not every "breaking" headline is actually significant, and that's not a conspiracy — it's how news aggregation naturally works when dozens of outlets compete to be first. A few practical signals worth checking before you treat a headline as important: does more than one independent outlet report it, not just outlets that share a wire feed? Does the story name specific, checkable numbers and sources rather than "officials say" or "sources suggest"? Has the story changed meaningfully since it was first posted (a sign it was published before all the facts were confirmed)?

On this site specifically: our "Breaking" section deliberately casts a wide net across multiple providers with light filtering, precisely because breaking news is noisy by nature. Our category sections (Business, Tech, Sports, etc.) apply stricter topic filtering. If you want signal over noise, the category pages are usually the better starting point than Breaking.
